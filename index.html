How Does the Electoral College Work?

The Electoral College is the method used to elect the US President and Vice President. It is not a location or a place of higher education, as it sounds, but an understanding between a vote in Congress and by a popular vote of legally registered voters in the United States. Our founding fathers established the Electoral College in the Constitution so that less populated states have just as much a voice in the presidential election as do more populated states. When we go to the polls on the second Tuesday after the first Monday in November every four years, we are in essence casting our vote for a group of electors to vote for a specific candidate, technically making this an indirect election. Each state is assigned electors that are equal to its number of representatives in the House plus two senators. Over the years, the number of representatives has changed based on population. The District of Columbia has three electors. Some states have split electoral votes like Maine and Nebraska where they go by congressional district. 270 out of 538 electoral votes are needed to win the presidency.

Is it true that we should eliminate the Electoral College due to the changing times? We seem to know more about our candidates than our electors. Is it fair to continue to elect someone for the most powerful position in the world the way our country always has, or do we have room for reform? Is it reasonable to continue making sure that smaller states are not left out of the decision for President or should we learn something from the fact that an American presidential candidate has lost five times due to winning the popular vote but losing the electoral college?

When thinking of the Electoral College it helps to remember that it balances power between large and small states so that the smaller ones have a voice in presidential elections. If the Electoral College did not exist the presidential candidates would most likely focus only on the larger states like California, Florida, Texas, or New York. The smaller states would be ignored and feel as if their vote did not matter and would merely be forgotten across an unfair election system. This way the attention is distributed evenly across the country. Another good point to know is that it makes it difficult for a smaller party candidate to gain electoral votes and encourages the original formation and continuation of two major political parties. Since candidates must appeal to a wider range of states and communities rather than just concentrating on the large states, voters may be less confused about the options and issues at hand. It also promotes the importance of voting and how the outcome is heavily affected by voters in swing states, who would be ignored in a direct popular voting system. The Electoral College is a familiar system and has been used for over two hundred years. We have seen it through nearly 100% peaceful transitions of power.

With the good always comes the bad. Some of the downsides of the Electoral College include the possibility of winning the popular vote but losing the election. This has happened five times in American history. In the year 2000, Al Gore won the popular vote and George W. Bush won the electoral votes needed to win the presidency. Opponents argue that the Electoral College was meant to protect smaller and rural states from being overpowered by urban areas with large populations. In recent years it has been noticed that presidential candidates are focused on “swing states,” those states that are almost divided right down the middle and can go either way. This leaves voters in other states feeling left out. Another con of the Electoral College is that it discourages voter turnout. If there is a state that usually is heavily Democrat or Republican, some people may feel that their vote does not count, and they may not vote. We could become more educated as a nation on how this process works so that we don’t remain so evenly divided on it. There are many opponents of our process of electing a President and some agree that our founding fathers could not figure out any other way to make it work.

In the last twenty-five years we have had two cases where a presidential candidate won the presidency but lost the popular vote. In the year 2000 it was George W. Bush and Al Gore both running for President and the whole election came down to one battleground state: Florida! At first the television networks declared Gore the winner then retracted and stated that Bush was the winner. Due to the fact that the margin of the vote was less than one-half of a percent of the number of votes in Florida, a recount was legitimately allowed to occur. After the recount Bush was still declared the winner however the margin of the vote was less than before. Gore applied for a manual recount in selected counties that were qualified under this provision. The deadline for recounting kept getting pushed out further and further into late November. After many speeches, appeals and filed objections, and contested ballots that the Florida Supreme Court failed to identify, it all came down to the US Supreme Court issuing the Florida Supreme Court a deadline of December 12, 2000, for creating standards for this recount. During this process, the manual recounts were stopped. On the day of this deadline the US Supreme Court validated the original report, and Bush was declared the winner of Florida’s twenty-five electoral votes and the 2000 presidential election.
